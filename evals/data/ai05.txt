An enzyme is a biological catalyst that accelerates chemical reactions within living systems without being consumed in the process. Most enzymes are proteins, although a small number of RNA molecules, known as ribozymes, also show catalytic activity. Enzymes bind specific substrates at an active site, lowering the activation energy a reaction requires and often increasing its rate by many orders of magnitude. Their specificity comes from the three-dimensional shape and chemical environment of that site. The International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology classifies enzymes into six major classes (oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, and ligases) using Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers. Activity depends on temperature, pH, substrate concentration, cofactors, and inhibitors, and cells regulate enzymes tightly to coordinate metabolism. The term was coined by Wilhelm Kühne in 1877, and enzymes are now central to medicine, industrial processing, and molecular biology.
