Coral reefs are underwater ecosystems built mainly by colonies of tiny animals called coral polyps, which secrete calcium carbonate skeletons that accumulate over thousands of years into vast limestone structures. They form chiefly in warm, shallow, clear, sunlit tropical waters, where symbiotic algae called zooxanthellae supply much of the coral's energy through photosynthesis. Although reefs cover less than 1 percent of the ocean floor, they support roughly a quarter of all known marine species, providing food, shelter, and nursery habitat for fish, mollusks, crustaceans, and many other organisms. Reefs also buffer coastlines from wave energy and sustain fisheries and tourism worth billions of dollars annually. Because corals are highly sensitive to temperature, acidification, sedimentation, and pollution, prolonged heat stress can trigger coral bleaching, in which corals expel their algae and may die. Many reefs worldwide are now in decline.
