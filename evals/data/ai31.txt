Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. It usually attacks the lungs but can also affect the kidneys, spine, and brain. It spreads through the air when people with active pulmonary disease cough, sneeze, or speak. Many infected people have latent TB, in which the bacteria remain dormant and cause no symptoms. Active disease brings a persistent cough, weight loss, fever, and night sweats. Humans have suffered from TB since antiquity, but Robert Koch identified the causative organism in 1882. Standard treatment combines several antibiotics over at least six months, though drug-resistant strains complicate control efforts. The BCG vaccine offers partial protection, mainly against severe childhood forms. The World Health Organization reports that TB remains one of the leading infectious causes of death worldwide, with about 10 million people falling ill each year.
