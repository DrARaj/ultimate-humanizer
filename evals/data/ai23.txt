The Atacama Desert occupies a narrow strip of northern Chile, stretching roughly 1,000 kilometres between the Pacific Ocean and the western slopes of the Andes and covering about 105,000 square kilometres. It is widely regarded as the driest non-polar desert on Earth, with some weather stations recording no rainfall for decades. Its extreme aridity results from the Andes' rain-shadow effect, the cold Humboldt Current offshore, and a persistent temperature inversion that suppresses cloud formation. Coastal fog, known as camanchaca, nonetheless supports sparse, specialised vegetation and lichens. The region holds rich nitrate and copper deposits, and its mines are today a major global source of copper and lithium. Its high altitude, dry air and clear skies also make it one of the world's premier sites for astronomy, home to observatories such as the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA). Because parts of its terrain resemble the Martian surface, researchers also use it as a planetary analogue.
