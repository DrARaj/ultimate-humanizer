Superconductivity is a set of physical properties observed in certain materials, in which electrical resistance vanishes and magnetic flux fields are expelled from the material below a characteristic critical temperature. It was first discovered by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911, in mercury cooled with liquid helium. The phenomenon was explained microscopically by the BCS theory of 1957, which describes electrons bound into Cooper pairs by lattice vibrations (phonons). Superconductors are classified as Type I or Type II according to their response to magnetic fields. Type II materials admit partial flux penetration in vortex states and can carry high currents in strong fields. Conventional superconductors typically operate only a few kelvin above absolute zero. The discovery of cuprate superconductors in 1986 raised critical temperatures above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen, and the highest ambient-pressure cuprates exceed 130 K, although their pairing mechanism remains incompletely understood. Applications include MRI magnets, particle accelerators, maglev trains, and power transmission cables.
