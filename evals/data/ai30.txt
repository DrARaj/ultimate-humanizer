Alan Mathison Turing (1912–1954) was a British mathematician, logician, and computer scientist whose work laid the theoretical foundations of modern computing. In his 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers," he introduced an abstract machine, now called the Turing machine, that formalized the notion of algorithmic computation. During the Second World War he worked at Bletchley Park, where he played a central role in breaking the German Enigma cipher. After the war he helped develop early stored-program computers and, in 1950, proposed the "imitation game," now known as the Turing test, as a criterion for machine intelligence. Turing was prosecuted in 1952 for homosexual acts, then a criminal offence in the United Kingdom, and died in 1954. He was posthumously pardoned in 2013. His ideas remain central to computer science, artificial intelligence, and cryptography.
