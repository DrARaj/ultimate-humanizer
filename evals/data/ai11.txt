The Roman Republic was the state that governed Rome from the overthrow of its last kings around 509 BCE until Augustus established the Principate in 27 BCE. Power was divided among elected magistrates, most notably two annually chosen consuls, and balanced by the Senate, an advisory body of former magistrates, and by popular assemblies in which citizens voted on laws and elected officials. The Struggle of the Orders, a long contest between patricians and plebeians, gradually opened high office to commoners and produced institutions such as the tribunes of the plebs, whose veto protected ordinary citizens. Rome expanded dramatically during this period, conquering Italy and then the Mediterranean world, including Carthage in the Punic Wars. Conquest brought wealth, slaves, and social strain, and the resulting political violence, from the Gracchan reforms through the civil wars of Marius, Sulla, Caesar, and Pompey, ultimately ended the Republic. Its institutions and ideas profoundly shaped later Western political thought.
