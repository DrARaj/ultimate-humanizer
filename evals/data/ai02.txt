Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that Earth's rigid outer shell, the lithosphere, is divided into a number of large plates that move slowly over the more ductile asthenosphere beneath them. Developed in the 1960s from earlier ideas such as continental drift, the theory unified observations including the global distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes, the matching shapes of continental coastlines, and symmetrical magnetic striping on the seafloor. Plates interact at three main boundary types: divergent boundaries, where new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges; convergent boundaries, where plates collide and one may subduct beneath another, producing trenches, mountain ranges, and volcanic arcs; and transform boundaries, where plates slide past one another, generating earthquakes. Driving forces include mantle convection and slab pull at subducting margins. Plate motion occurs at rates of a few centimeters per year, and over geological time it has assembled and broken apart supercontinents such as Pangaea, shaping climate, ocean circulation, and the distribution of life.
