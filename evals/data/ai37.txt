Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal infection caused by ingesting food or water contaminated with *Vibrio cholerae*, chiefly the O1 and O139 serogroups that produce cholera toxin. The toxin drives profuse loss of water and electrolytes from the small intestine, causing rapid dehydration that can prove fatal within hours if untreated, although most infections are mild or asymptomatic. The disease remains endemic in parts of Asia, Africa, and the Americas, where poor sanitation, crowding, and unsafe water sustain outbreaks, often amplified by conflict, displacement, and natural disasters. Treatment centres on oral rehydration solution, with intravenous fluids for severe cases; antibiotics shorten illness and reduce shedding. Vaccination, safe water, and improved sanitation form the main prevention strategy. Cholera also holds a notable place in the history of epidemiology: John Snow's 1854 study of a London outbreak, which traced cases to a contaminated water pump, helped establish waterborne transmission.
