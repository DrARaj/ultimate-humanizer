Marie Curie (born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw, 1867; died 1934) was a Polish-born physicist and chemist who became one of the most influential scientists of the modern era. Working in Paris with her husband, Pierre Curie, she pioneered research on radioactivity, a term she helped establish, and isolated two new elements, polonium, named after her homeland, and radium. She shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel, becoming the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize. In 1911 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, making her the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. She was also the first woman to become a professor at the University of Paris and founded the Radium Institute. Her prolonged exposure to radiation likely caused the aplastic anemia from which she died in 1934.
