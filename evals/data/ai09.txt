The periodic table is a tabular arrangement of the chemical elements, ordered by atomic number so that elements with similar properties fall into shared vertical columns called groups. Its horizontal rows, called periods, reflect the progressive filling of electron shells. Early versions appeared in the 1860s and 1870s, when Dmitri Mendeleev and Lothar Meyer noticed that elements sorted by atomic mass displayed recurring patterns. Mendeleev boldly left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties. Henry Moseley's X-ray work in 1913 established atomic number as the proper organizing principle. The table now lists 118 confirmed elements, the most recent named in 2016, and divides them into metals, metalloids, and nonmetals. Its enduring value lies in showing how atomic structure governs chemical behavior, and chemists, physicists, and other scientists rely on it to predict reactivity, bonding, and physical properties.
