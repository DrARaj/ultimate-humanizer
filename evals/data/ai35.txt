Penicillin is a group of β-lactam antibiotics derived from *Penicillium* fungi. Its antibacterial activity was first noticed in 1928 by Scottish bacteriologist Alexander Fleming at St Mary's Hospital in London, who saw that a mold contaminating a culture plate had destroyed the surrounding staphylococci. Fleming's finding went largely unexploited until Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and Norman Heatley at Oxford purified the compound and developed methods to produce it in the late 1930s and early 1940s. Mass production, pursued chiefly in the United States during the Second World War, made penicillin the first widely used antibiotic and saved many lives among wounded soldiers. The three men shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Penicillin kills bacteria by inhibiting cell wall synthesis, binding to penicillin-binding proteins. Its widespread use has also driven bacterial resistance, notably through β-lactamase enzymes that break the drug down.
