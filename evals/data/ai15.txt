The Peace of Westphalia refers to a series of treaties signed in 1648 at Osnabrück and Münster in the Holy Roman Empire, ending the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic. Negotiated by delegates from most major European powers, the settlements recognized the independence of the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederation, granted France territories including Alsace, and gave Sweden western Pomerania and other lands in northern Germany. Within the Empire, the princes gained greater autonomy, including the right to conclude alliances, while the emperor's authority was curtailed. The religious provisions extended legal recognition to Calvinism alongside Lutheranism and Catholicism and fixed confessional boundaries by reference to 1624. Although later scholars have debated whether the treaties inaugurated a modern system of sovereign, equal states, they are often cited as foundational to the principles of state sovereignty and non-interference in domestic affairs that shaped European diplomacy thereafter.
