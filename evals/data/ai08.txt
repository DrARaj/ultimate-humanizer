Gravitational waves are ripples in the curvature of spacetime produced by accelerating massive objects, such as orbiting black holes or colliding neutron stars. Albert Einstein predicted them in 1916 as a consequence of his general theory of relativity, but they were long thought too weak to detect because the strain they produce is minute. The first direct detection came on 14 September 2015, when the LIGO observatories in the United States registered the merger of two black holes about 1.3 billion light-years away. Since then, LIGO, the Virgo detector in Italy, and KAGRA in Japan have cataloged dozens of similar events. Gravitational waves travel at the speed of light and interact only weakly with matter, so they can carry information from regions that are opaque to light. Their study has created gravitational-wave astronomy, a field that complements traditional telescopes and provides new tests of general relativity.
