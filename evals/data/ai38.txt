Smallpox is an acute, highly contagious infectious disease caused by the variola virus, a member of the genus *Orthopoxvirus* in the family Poxviridae. It spreads mainly via respiratory droplets and, less often, contact with lesions or contaminated materials. After an incubation period of about 12 days, the infection produced fever, severe body aches, and a characteristic rash that progressed to fluid-filled pustules and scarring. The most severe form, variola major, killed roughly 30 percent of those infected. The disease killed hundreds of millions of people over the centuries. Edward Jenner's 1796 demonstration that inoculation with cowpox protected against smallpox laid the foundation for vaccination. A global campaign led by the World Health Organization eventually eradicated the disease; the last naturally occurring case was recorded in Somalia in 1977, and the WHO certified eradication in 1980. Today, variola virus stocks are held only in two high-security laboratories, in the United States and Russia.
