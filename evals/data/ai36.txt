Vaccination is the administration of a vaccine to stimulate an individual's adaptive immune system, conferring protection against a specific infectious disease without causing the illness itself. Vaccines contain antigens derived from a pathogen, presented in weakened, inactivated, partial, or genetically encoded form, which prompt the body to produce memory B and T cells. The principle originated with Edward Jenner's 1796 demonstration that exposure to cowpox protected against smallpox. Immunisation programmes expanded markedly in the twentieth century, leading the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980 and contributing to sharp declines in diphtheria, measles, and polio. Beyond individual protection, widespread vaccination generates herd immunity, shielding people who cannot be vaccinated, such as infants and the immunocompromised. Vaccines undergo rigorous clinical trials and ongoing safety surveillance, and they are widely regarded as among the most cost-effective public health interventions in history.
