The Silk Road was a network of overland and maritime trade routes linking East Asia with the Mediterranean world, active from roughly the 2nd century BCE to the 15th century CE. The name, coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877, refers to the Chinese silk carried westward, but exchange ran in both directions and included spices, precious metals, paper, horses, and ideas. Caravans crossed the deserts and mountains of Central Asia, linking oasis cities such as Dunhuang, Kashgar, Samarkand, and Bukhara, while merchants and pilgrims carried Buddhism, Islam, and Nestorian Christianity along the same paths. Sea routes through the Indian Ocean complemented the land network. Under the Mongol Empire in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the Pax Mongolica offered unusual security for long-distance travel. The routes declined as Ottoman power grew, maritime shipping expanded, and plague spread along them. Today the term is often used more broadly for cultural and economic connectivity across Eurasia.
