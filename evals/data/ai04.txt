A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so intense that nothing, not even light, can escape once it crosses the boundary known as the event horizon. Predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity, black holes form when massive stars collapse at the end of their lives, and they range from stellar-mass objects of a few to tens of solar masses to supermassive ones containing millions or billions of Suns, which are thought to reside at the centers of most large galaxies. Because they emit no light, they are detected indirectly, through the motion of nearby stars, glowing disks of infalling gas, and gravitational waves from merging black holes. The Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of a black hole's shadow, of the central object in galaxy M87, in 2019. Stephen Hawking's theoretical work further predicted that black holes slowly emit thermal radiation, linking gravity, quantum mechanics, and thermodynamics.
